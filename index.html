What the problem actually is

Four real problems. Four real fixes. No scare tactics.

Your water isn't poison, and anyone telling you that is selling something. But "safe to drink" and "good water" are different standards, and a few things are genuinely worth fixing.

01The problem

Lead gets in after the testing stops

Your utility tests the water it sends down the street. It can't test the pipe running into your house, or the solder in your walls. If those are old, lead enters in the last few feet of the journey — and there's no level of lead considered safe for children, who absorb it far more readily than adults do.

The fix: Certified carbon filtration — at the kitchen tap, or whole-home if you want it everywhere. Neither requires repiping your house.
02The problem

Chlorine keeps water safe, then keeps going

Chlorine is why municipal water doesn't make you sick, so nobody should want it gone at the plant. But it stays in the water all the way to your glass, along with the byproducts that form when it reacts with organic matter. Those are regulated and monitored — and they're also why your water tastes like a swimming pool.

The fix: Carbon reduces chlorine, its byproducts, and the taste that comes with them — at the point where it stops doing its job and starts being a nuisance.
03The problem

Hard water is quietly expensive

At 7 to 13 grains per gallon, this region's water leaves scale on everything it touches. It coats the element in your water heater so it burns more energy for the same hot shower, shortens the life of every appliance plumbed into it, and makes you use noticeably more soap for the same result.

The fix: A properly sized softener. Not a health purchase — an appliance-life and utility-bill one that pays back slowly and permanently.
04The problem

Well water answers to nobody

No utility, no annual report, no requirement to test after the well is drilled. Arsenic occurs naturally in the bedrock under Washtenaw County — the USGS estimates roughly a one in eight chance a well here exceeds the federal limit, and it has no taste, smell or color. Coliform and nitrates are equally invisible, and nitrates matter most for infants.

The fix: Test first, always. Then a system sized to your actual results — not a package sold to every well in the county.

Where you're getting your water

Two very different starting points.

Plymouth & Canton

Municipal water, GLWA-sourced

Your water is treated and safe to drink — the issue is hardness (7–13 grains per gallon) and chlorine taste, not safety. This is a comfort and appliance-longevity fix, not an emergency.

Salem Township & well-served areas

Private wells, untested by default

Nobody regulates or tests a private well but you. Some problems (iron, sulfur) you can smell. Others (coliform, nitrates, arsenic) you can't — which is exactly why we test before we ever recommend a system.
